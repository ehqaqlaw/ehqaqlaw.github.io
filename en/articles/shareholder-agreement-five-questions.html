The Shareholder Agreement: Five Questions to Settle Before Incorporation

Most disputes between partners are not about money but about a question that was never asked at the start. Five questions to answer in writing before the commercial register is issued.

Reviewed by the Ehqaq Law team · Last updated: 18 September 2026 · Related service: Companies, Partnerships & Governance

The articles of association registered with the competent authorities answer the law's questions: who the partners are, what the capital is, who the manager is. They do not usually answer the partners' own questions: what happens if one of them cannot inject their share of the funding? Who decides if they split down the middle? How does someone who wants to leave get out? These are the questions for a shareholder agreement, and the best time to answer them is when there is no dispute.

1. Who owns what, and why?

Shares correspond to what each partner contributes: money, effort, an idea, or relationships. The dispute begins when one contributes money and another time, and they then disagree about the value of the time. Write down the basis on which the shares were calculated, and what happens if a promised contribution is not made: is the share reduced, is the excess treated as a loan to the company, or does a new partner come in? If a sweat-equity share is earned gradually, define its period and conditions.

2. Who decides, and what happens in a deadlock?

Day-to-day decisions belong to the manager, major decisions to the partners. But which are major? Define them in a list: changing the activity, borrowing above a threshold, selling a major asset, admitting a partner, distributing or withholding profits, and appointing and removing the manager. Then set the approval threshold for each. Most importantly: what happens if the votes are tied or a decision is blocked? Referral to a third party, a mutual buy-out right, or a negotiation period followed by an exit mechanism. Unresolved deadlock kills successful companies.

3. Who works in the company, and for what?

A partner who works full time needs remuneration for their work separate from their share of profits; otherwise they feel they are funding the others with their time. Define who works, with what time commitment, at what salary or bonus, whether other work is permitted, the limits on competing with the company during and after the partnership, and what happens to their share if they stop working.

4. How does a partner leave?

Exit always happens, by sale, by dispute, by death or by incapacity. An agreement without an exit door keeps a partner captive or brings the others a partner they did not choose. Define: the partners' right of first refusal on a sale of shares, how the shares are valued and by whom, the other partners' right to join a sale when one of them receives a good offer or to require the minority to sell when the whole company is sold, what happens to the share on death or serious breach, and a payment schedule for the share price if it is not paid in cash.

5. How are disputes resolved?

Not every dispute is a lawsuit. Set out a ladder: direct negotiation within a set period, then a mediator chosen by both sides, then the final forum: the competent court or arbitration. Each has its cost and procedure; arbitration is faster and more private in some cases but more expensive, and may not suit a small company. Whatever the choice, define who runs the company during the dispute so that the business does not stop.

How the agreement relates to the articles of association

The shareholder agreement is a private contract binding between its parties; the articles of association are a published statutory document that binds third parties. The two should complement, not contradict, each other: approval thresholds or restrictions on share transfers set in the agreement are best reflected in the articles to the extent the Companies Law allows, so that a partner never faces two documents saying different things.

Checklist

  1. The basis for calculating shares, and the consequence of a partner failing to contribute.
  2. The list of major decisions and the approval threshold for each.
  3. A deadlock-breaking mechanism.
  4. The working partner: commitment, remuneration and non-competition limits.
  5. Right of first refusal and the valuation method on a sale.
  6. Tag-along and drag-along rights.
  7. Death, incapacity and serious breach, and their effect on the share.
  8. The dispute-resolution ladder and final forum, and management during a dispute.
  9. Consistency between the agreement and the articles of association.
Setting up a company, or operating without an agreement?

We draft the shareholder agreement to fit your actual relationship and review the articles of association alongside it so the two complement each other, with a scope and fee you know before you commit. If the company already exists, a later agreement is possible as long as the partners agree.
