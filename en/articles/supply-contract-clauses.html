Clauses Often Overlooked in Supply and Service Contracts

A contract is written for the day the parties disagree, not the day they agree. Twelve clauses forgotten in the rush to sign that later create the dispute.

Reviewed by the Ehqaq Law team · Last updated: 18 September 2026 · Related service: Contracts & Agreements

Most supply and service contracts that reach the courts are not contracts without clauses. They are contracts with many clauses about the things the parties agreed easily, and few or none about the things they assumed would never happen. These are the clauses we most often see missing, and what exactly is missing from them.

1. Scope and acceptance criteria

Describing what is to be supplied or provided is not enough; what matters is when delivery counts as accepted: against which specifications, who inspects, within what period objections may be raised, and what happens if the period passes without objection. Without an acceptance criterion, every delivery can be disputed months later.

2. The delivery schedule and the effect of delay

Set the dates as calendar dates or as periods running from a clear event, and set the effect of delay: a penalty at a known rate for each period of delay, a cap on it, and the right to terminate once delay reaches a certain point. An undefined or uncapped penalty is a source of dispute for both sides.

3. Payments and their triggers

Link each payment to a provable event: signing, accepted delivery or an approved progress statement, not to a bare date. Set the payment period after the invoice, the effect of late payment, and whether the supplier may suspend supply when payment is late.

4. Variations and additional work

Most service and construction disputes turn on work done on an oral request whose payment was later refused. Require that no change to scope, price or time be recognised except by a written variation order signed by the authorised persons, and identify those persons by name or position.

5. Warranty and after delivery

The warranty period, what it covers, how defects are notified, the period for repair or replacement, and who bears transport costs. For services: the period of post-delivery support and its limits.

6. Termination

Distinguish termination for breach from termination for convenience on notice, and set the consequences of each: what is paid for work performed, what happens to materials purchased, and handover of what has been completed. A contract without a clear termination clause keeps both parties bound to a contract one of them no longer wants.

7. Liability, its limits, and insurance

Is each party's liability capped? Are indirect losses excluded? Who insures the goods in transit and the works during execution? These clauses are usually read only once: after the loss has occurred.

8. Confidentiality and ownership of deliverables

In service contracts especially: who owns the designs, reports and software produced? When does ownership pass: on delivery or on full payment? And what are the limits on each party's use of the other's information after the contract ends?

9. Force majeure

Define it narrowly, require notice within a set period, and set its effect: an extension of time rather than release from the obligation, and a right to terminate if it continues. A general clause that excuses everything in every circumstance empties the contract of meaning.

10. Notices and addresses

Designate an email address and a postal address for each party, and treat what is sent to them as duly notified. Many defences in court turn on “I never received the notice.”

11. Dispute resolution

Amicable settlement within a set period first, then the competent court or arbitration. Do not include an arbitration clause because it looks professional; arbitration has its own costs and procedures and may not suit a low-value contract. If you do choose it, specify the seat, the language, the number of arbitrators and the administering institution.

12. The governing language

If the contract is in two languages, state that the Arabic text prevails in case of conflict; Arabic is the language of litigation in the Kingdom, and whatever is left unstated becomes a dispute at the first translation.

Checklist before signing

  1. Acceptance criterion and objection period.
  2. Delay penalty, its cap, and the right to terminate.
  3. Payments linked to provable events, and the payment period.
  4. A written variation-order mechanism and who signs it.
  5. Warranty: period, scope and procedure.
  6. Termination: types and consequences.
  7. Liability cap and insurance.
  8. Ownership of deliverables and confidentiality.
  9. Force majeure: definition, notice and effect.
  10. Designated notice addresses.
  11. Amicable settlement, then the competent forum.
  12. The language that prevails in case of conflict.
