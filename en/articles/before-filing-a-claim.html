Before You File a Claim: What Do You Need to Document?

A judge rules on what is proven, not on what is told. A practical list of what to gather before you make a claim or file a lawsuit, and how to preserve it in a form that will count.

Reviewed by the Ehqaq Law team · Last updated: 18 September 2026 · Related service: Financial Claims & Commercial Disputes

What weakens financial claims most is rarely the weakness of the right itself, but the weakness of its proof. The claimant knows in detail what happened; the judge knows only what is put before them, and the burden of proving a claim lies on the party bringing it. Serious work on any claim therefore begins long before court: gathering what proves the relationship, what proves performance, and what proves the demand.

First: what proves there is an obligation

The starting point is a written contract signed by both parties. Where no single complete contract exists, there are usually documents that together take its place: a quotation accepted in writing, a purchase order, or correspondence agreeing scope, price and dates. Gather all of them in chronological order, and do not assume the other party will admit what you agreed orally.

  • The contract or agreement, with all its signed schedules and amendments.
  • Quotations, purchase orders and whatever proves their acceptance.
  • Correspondence that defines or changes the scope: emails, messages and meeting minutes.
  • The other party's official details: its name as in the commercial register, registration number, address, and who signed on its behalf and in what capacity.

Second: what proves that you performed

A claim for the price, the rent or a progress payment presupposes that you delivered or completed. Prove it with documents issued or approved by the other party, not with your own documents alone.

  • Signed delivery and receipt notes, or delivery notices confirmed by the carrier.
  • Progress statements or completion reports approved by the other party or its consultant.
  • The tax invoices issued, and proof that they were received.
  • A record of payments received and the balance clearly calculated, with proof of any agreed deduction.

Third: what proves that you demanded payment

A demand made before litigation shows that the other party knew of the debt and delayed with that knowledge, and shows your good faith in seeking an amicable solution. A written formal notice with proof of delivery, stating a specific amount, a clear due date and a reasonable period for payment, is in many disputes the turning point from a loose demand to a claim that is ready.

Electronic correspondence: how to preserve it so that it counts

Electronic records and correspondence are admissible in evidence under the Evidence Law, subject to the conditions it sets, including the ability to verify their source and integrity. Therefore:

  • Do not delete conversations or messages, even those that seem unfavourable; selective deletion weakens the weight of what remains.
  • Keep the original on the device or account, not screenshots alone; a screenshot is a weak substitute that is easy to challenge.
  • Export complete conversations with their dates from the application itself, and keep a backup.
  • Note the mobile numbers and email addresses used in the correspondence and who owns them; the link between a number and its owner is the first thing that gets argued.

Witnesses and expert evidence

Some facts cannot be proven by documents alone: the condition of goods on delivery, the quality of work, or what was said in a meeting. Identify who was present and can testify, and keep their contact details. If the matter is technical, such as conformity with specifications or valuing work done, be aware that the court may appoint an expert, and that an early technical report from an independent body may save you time and money.

Deadlines: do not wait

The law sets periods after which certain claims will not be accepted; they vary with the nature of the claim and the competent forum, and may run from the date the right arose or from the date of the last demand or acknowledgment. Do not rely on your own assumptions about these periods; consult a lawyer early, because some claims are lost simply by delay.

Checklist

  1. The contract and its schedules, or the quotations, orders and correspondence that take its place.
  2. The other party's official details and who signed for it.
  3. Proof of delivery or completion, issued or approved by the other party.
  4. Invoices, the payment record and the balance.
  5. Complete correspondence in its original form, exported and saved.
  6. The formal notice or earlier demand and proof of its delivery.
  7. Witnesses' names and contact details.
  8. A one-page timeline of the key facts, their dates, and the document for each.
When do you need a lawyer?

When the amount justifies it, when you feel that some of these documents are missing and you do not know how to make up for them, or when the other party's reply disputes the principle and not just the amount. The initial claim assessment answers one question before you pay litigation fees: is this file worth bringing, and by which route?
