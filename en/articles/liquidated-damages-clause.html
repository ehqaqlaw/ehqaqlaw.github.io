The Penalty Clause: When It Helps You, and When It Cannot Be Relied On

The parties write it to avoid a dispute over the amount of compensation, and then it becomes the dispute itself. What makes an agreed-damages clause actually enforceable, and what strips it of its effect.

Reviewed by the Ehqaq Law team · Last updated: 18 September 2026 · Related service: Contracts & Agreements

A penalty clause, or agreed compensation, is an advance agreement between the contracting parties on the compensation due when a specific obligation is breached, instead of leaving its assessment to the judge after the breach. Its purpose is to protect both parties: the creditor knows what they will receive without having to prove the amount of their loss, and the debtor knows the limit of their exposure. But the clause does not operate automatically, as many assume, and understanding its limits matters more than drafting it.

What the clause does, and what it does not do

The clause relieves the creditor of proving the amount of loss, not of proving the breach itself. The creditor must still show that the debtor breached the obligation to which the clause is tied, and that the breach is attributable to the debtor rather than to an external cause. Loss is then presumed, and it remains open to the debtor to rebut that presumption.

When it may not be relied on

The law treats agreed compensation as compensation, not as punishment, and its limits follow from that. In general terms:

  • If the debtor proves that the creditor suffered no loss at all, the agreed compensation may not be due.
  • If the debtor proves that the assessment was grossly excessive, or that the original obligation was performed in part, the court may reduce the amount accordingly.
  • Conversely, the creditor may not claim more than the agreed amount unless they prove fraud or gross fault on the debtor's part.
  • A clause tied to an unlawful obligation, or drafted so vaguely that the intended breach cannot be identified, undermines itself.

An exaggerated clause is therefore not stronger but weaker: it invites a challenge to its assessment, and may end in less compensation than could have been agreed realistically.

A delay penalty is not always a penalty clause

In contracts with government entities, delay penalties are governed by special rules that set their rates and caps, and they apply under those rules rather than by the parties' agreement. In contracts between private parties, an agreed delay penalty is a form of penalty clause, subject to the limits above. Distinguishing the two changes how the clause is drafted and how it is claimed.

How to draft a clause that holds

  1. Tie it to a specific breach: late delivery, non-conformity with specifications, or disclosure of confidential information, not “any breach of the contract.”
  2. Define the basis of calculation: a lump sum, or a percentage of the value of the delayed portion per day or week, stating what it is calculated on.
  3. Set a reasonable cap that reflects the loss actually expected; a realistic cap protects the clause from reduction.
  4. State its relationship to termination and to compensation for actual loss: whether it is due together with termination or instead of it, and whether more may be claimed in cases of fraud or gross fault.
  5. Require notice: a written notice of breach and a reasonable period to remedy before the clause is applied; this shows good faith and settles the argument over the start date.
  6. Document the loss despite the clause; when the assessment is challenged, the actual loss you documented is what protects the amount.

If you are the debtor

Do not sign a penalty clause whose basis of calculation and cap you have not read, and do not assume the court will reduce it; reduction requires proof from you. Ask for the penalty against you to be matched by equivalent obligations on the other party for their delay in payment or in delivering what your work depends on.
